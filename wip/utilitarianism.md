0 -- Basic personal thoughts.  

Utilitarianism is a theory we use as a criterion of rightness. We conceptualise the criterion as a well-ordering of all reachable states of reality by goodness, with the best one being the state one ought to reach. Reaching the highest-goodness state is the correct thing to do, and doing anything less than that is incorrect. It is a well-ordering, though, so some states are better than others, and actions can be more or less good.  

1 -- The demandingness objection.    

Quite a lot of people argue that utilitarianism is too demanding, with the theory leaving no room for actions that are permissible but not required.  
Our view is that if a theory of moral value just tells you to keep doing what you're already doing, it might not actually be a theory. We value those because they make novel predictions which are accurate, as it happens.  
Yes, utilitarianism is incredibly demanding. No perfect utilitarian has ever existed and one will never exist; perfect consequentialism is a stronger requirement than perfect Bayesian reasoning, which is known to be intractable.  
We just don't think those are issues. You can still try to approximate it, and you need to know what you're approximating to have any hope of consistently getting decent results.

5 -- Distribution insensivity.   

A common objection is that utilitarianism does not care about fairness -- we can have a world where some people have 80 utils and some people have 20 utils, split equally, that utilitarianism assigns a higher goodness to than one which has everyone possessing 45 utils.  
This line of thought does appeal to some real intuitions, not that we think intuitions should be guiding moral reasoning.  
Still, we have some responses.  

  a -- Utilitarians do not assign a terminal value to equality, but we absolutely care about it instrumentally. In practice, we seem to care about it a lot more than other moral philosophies do. With a given amount of resources, the good-maximising distribution is generally to those who are the least well-off, because you benefit more from resources the fewer of them you have. Implementations of utilitarianism have a natural tendency of becoming egalitarian. This makes equality very, very important.
  b -- You should bite the bullet. The first world looks straightforwardly better. I don't think you can justify taking X utils from someone to give nine-tenths of that to another person, such a preference seems straightforwardly incoherent.
